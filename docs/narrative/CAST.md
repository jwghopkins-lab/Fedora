# GROUND TRUTH — the cast

Phase 3. One entry per character kept, split three ways:

- **FACT** — checkable, physically present, true. Only these may be *nodes* a
  player stands on.
- **INTERPRETATION** — a reading, not a claim about the record. Cannot be
  refuted by search because there is nothing to refute. This is where the
  fiction lives.
- **INVENTED** — outright made up. May only ever be an *edge* between two
  facts, never a terminal.

The red-team result that forces this split is in `STATE.md`. Short version:
every fabrication style scored **zero** corroborations and the tell was
"unverifiable", not "false". So we never assert a fact we made up. We assert
facts that are true, and we *read* them.

## Cast size

**Six humans on the route, two objects that behave like characters, two people
who exist only in the document.** The rule that matters is not the count:

> **Every character must appear at least twice. At least one must appear in all
> three acts.**

A character seen once is scenery. A character seen three times is evidence.
The feeling of a pattern — which is the entire emotional payload of a
conspiracy story — comes from recurrence, not from headcount. A large cast each
appearing once produces a walk that feels like a list. Six who keep coming back
produces a walk that feels like something is going on.

## Belsky — the twentieth-century continuer

*The strongest thread in the game, and almost all of it is true.*

**Where:** Mountbatten Green off Horse Guards Road (Act 1) and the north
terrace of Trafalgar Square (Act 2). He is on the walk twice, in both acts,
which is why he carries the spine.

### FACT
- Franta Belsky, born Brno 1921, came to Britain as a refugee in 1939, served
  in the Czechoslovak Brigade in the British Army.
- **It was his habit to seal inside each of his castings a Guinness bottle, a
  copy of that day's newspaper, a sixpence, and a note declaring that Franta
  Belsky was the artist.** Not once. Every casting.
- **Cunningham bust**, Trafalgar Square north terrace, unveiled **2 April
  1967** by the Duke of Edinburgh — the first sculpture in the square by a
  foreign-born artist. A half-pint Guinness bottle and Belsky's note are inside
  it. It stands feet from the public imperial standards.
- **Mountbatten statue**, unveiled **2 November 1983**. **A jam jar containing
  coins, press cuttings and details of the commission is hidden in the left
  leg.** Cast by the **Meridian Bronze Foundry**.

### FACT — the dates around him
- **24 May 1965**: Douglas Jay tells the Commons that British industry will
  convert to metric sector by sector, aiming to finish by 1975. It is in
  Hansard.
- **1967**: the Decimal Currency Act. The sixpence is legislated toward
  extinction — in the same year Belsky seals one into a monument on the north
  terrace.
- **21 October 1983**: the 17th CGPM redefines the metre as the distance light
  travels in vacuum in 1/299,792,458 of a second. The metre stops being a bar
  you can hold and becomes a property of light.
- **2 November 1983**: twelve days later, Belsky's Mountbatten is unveiled,
  cast by a foundry called Meridian, with a jar of coins in its leg.

### INTERPRETATION
- A man who left his own name inside every bronze he made was doing more than
  signing it — a signature nobody can ever read is not a signature. The
  autograph was the cover story. The **contents** were the point.
- He seals a sixpence in the year the sixpence is legislated away. He seals
  coins again twelve days after the metre stops being a thing you can hold. He
  was not making time capsules. He was **depositing units somewhere they could
  not be legislated out of existence.**
- A refugee from a country that had just been measured off the map, doing this
  in London bronze, at exactly those two dates. Nobody can disprove the
  reading, because it is a reading.

### INVENTED
- That the 1967 note is not only a signature: that a marked length went into
  the Cunningham bust, and the note says what it is.
- That the clerk's survey book records the foundry habit, and the researcher's
  margin note is the one that joins it to Belsky.

### The honesty problem, and why it is actually the ending
**We cannot open a bust, and we must never invite anyone to try.** So "he
sealed a yard in there" can only ever be a claim inside the document. Players
will never verify it.

That is not a weakness. It is the resolution, and it falls straight out of D7
being locked:

> They cannot open the bust. So they measure the terrace instead — and the
> number they read off their own cord is the proof the bust was only ever the
> promise.

**The bust is the promise. The plate is the proof. Their hand does the work.**

**Hard rule:** no clue, hint or explainer may suggest touching, opening,
prising, tapping or otherwise interfering with any sculpture. The bust is to
be looked at.

## Lutyens — measurement that lies on purpose

**Where:** the Cenotaph, Whitehall (Act 1). The Trafalgar Square fountains
(Act 2). Two acts, no effort required.

### FACT
- The **Cenotaph has no straight lines.** Entasis derived from measurements of
  the Parthenon: every vertical is curved and inclined so the lines would meet
  about 1,000 ft in the air; every horizontal is an arc of a circle centred
  about 900 ft below the pavement. The calculations are said to have filled a
  manuscript book of thirty-three pages.
- He **remodelled the Trafalgar Square fountains, 1937–39**, as memorials to
  Beatty and Jellicoe. Bronzes finished 1939, work suspended by the war,
  unveiled by George VI in 1948.
- He was **Edwin Landseer Lutyens** — godson and namesake of the man who made
  the lions.

### INTERPRETATION
- The nation's monument to its dead is a structure in which no measurement is
  what it appears to be, and this was thought a *virtue*. The players are being
  taught, on a real object, that Britain knowingly builds things whose
  dimensions lie — and calls it refinement.
- Thirty-three pages of arithmetic to make a stone look honest.

### INVENTED
- Nothing yet. Lutyens does not need invention; he needs a clue that makes a
  player stand in Whitehall and realise the thing is bent.

## Turner — the eyewitness, and the light

**Where:** the 1834 fire (Act 1). Born at Maiden Lane, Covent Garden (Act 3).
He closes the loop the walk opens.

### FACT
- Born in **Maiden Lane, Covent Garden** — where the walk ends.
- He **watched the burning of the Houses of Parliament from the south bank on
  16 October 1834**, filled sketchbooks, and painted it twice.
- That fire destroyed the imperial standard yard.

### INTERPRETATION
- The best-documented eyewitness to the destruction of the national standard
  of length was a man who spent his life on the behaviour of light — and who
  was born four hundred yards from where the walk ends. He is the join between
  the two threads, and he is the join between the two ends of the route.

### INVENTED
- Nothing yet. Do not put words in his mouth or sketches in his hand that do
  not exist; the real sketchbooks are catalogued and someone will look.

## Ray Strachey — the 1884 table

**Where:** the Millicent Fawcett plinth, Parliament Square (Act 1, part 1).

### FACT
- Her name is on the plinth. So is **Henrietta Franklin's** — the clue reads a
  name off the stone, so which name we choose costs nothing to change.
- She married **Oliver Strachey** in 1911. Oliver was the son of **Lieut.-Gen.
  Sir Richard Strachey**, one of the four British commissioners at the **1884
  International Meridian Conference** and elected one of its secretaries — the
  table where Britain was handed the world's meridian on an expressed hope it
  would accede to the Convention du Mètre, and never did.
- **Oliver Strachey was a cryptographer** — MI1, then GC&CS, head of the ISOS
  section ("Illicit Services Oliver Strachey").

### INTERPRETATION
- The first name the players read, on the first clue, is two hops from the room
  where the meridian was traded. They will not know that for two hours.

### INVENTED
- Nothing. Use the connection as an **edge, never stated flatly** — a link by
  marriage reads as contrived when asserted and as uncanny when found.

## Landseer — the monument that is measurably wrong

**Where:** the lions, Trafalgar Square (Act 2).

### FACT
- The lions are **anatomically wrong**: domestic-cat paws, and backs that are
  concave where a lion's is convex.
- Why: the plaster cast promised from Turin was late, so he worked from zoo
  sketches; when a zoo lion died he got the carcass, and it decomposed faster
  than he could study it.
- Cast in bronze by Marochetti, unveiled 1867.
- Lutyens was his godson and namesake.

### INTERPRETATION
- Four of the most photographed objects in Britain are the wrong shape, the
  reason is on the record, and nobody minds. This is the walk's argument in
  miniature: **the country does not check, and when told, shrugs.**

### INVENTED
- Nothing. The truth is better.

## Verne — the day gained by crossing the line

**Where:** Mr Fogg's, Covent Garden (Act 3, already a location gate at part 18).

### FACT
- *Around the World in Eighty Days* (1872) turns entirely on **the day Fogg
  gains by travelling east across the meridian** — he thinks he has lost the
  wager and has not.
- Published **twelve years before** the conference that fixed that meridian at
  Greenwich.

### INTERPRETATION
- The most famous plot twist in Victorian fiction is a metrology joke: a man
  wins because the world cannot agree where the day starts. Verne wrote it
  before anyone had decided.

### INVENTED
- Nothing. Fogg is already fictional; do not build fiction on fiction.

## Nelson's Column — the number that was wrong for 150 years

**Where:** the middle of Act 2. An object, not a person, and it carries more
than most of the humans.

### FACT
- Believed to be **185 ft** for about a century and a half.
- The **2006 laser survey**, taken before refurbishment, found **169 ft 3 in —
  14 ft 6 in short**.

### INTERPRETATION
- A published national dimension was wrong by fourteen and a half feet, for a
  hundred and fifty years, in the middle of the capital, because nobody
  measured it. If that is true of the column, ask what else was never
  re-measured. This is the clean, undeniable, entirely factual statement of the
  game's premise, and it should be the moment the walk turns.

### INVENTED
- Nothing, ever. This one must stay pristine — it is the load-bearing fact that
  makes everything else feel possible.

## The terrace plates — where the cord goes

### FACT
- Public imperial standards set into the north terrace in 1876, inscribed **"at
  62 degrees Fahrenheit"**, triplicated with the Guildhall and the Greenwich
  gate. **Moved east in 2003.**
- **No source states whether the present plates are the 1876 originals or
  recuts.** Every account stops short of it.

### INTERPRETATION
- The players stand on the one place in the story where the record genuinely
  runs out — and they run out with it, in person, holding a cord.

### INVENTED
- Whatever the cord says when they lay it down. That number is ours.

## The clerk — the Victorian hand *(D5)*

An invented man in a real office: **vestry clerk of St Martin-in-the-Fields**,
corresponding with Smyth in the 1880s, his survey book ending in **1900** when
he is told to hand over the parish's standards and does not. Smyth died in
February 1900 and the vestries were abolished the same year, so the dating
locks without being forced.

**He dies in the Spanish flu** *(client, 15 Aug)*. This is the right pick over
the war: he would be past sixty by 1918, so a war death needs explaining and a
flu death does not. It also means the book stops for a reason that is not
sinister — which leaves the reader to decide whether that is a relief. Nobody
takes a statement from him, because in that autumn nobody was taking
statements.

Note the eighteen-year gap: the **book** ends in 1900, the **man** ends in
1918. The gap is usable, not a hole — it is why the book was in a cupboard
rather than an archive.

## The researcher — the modern hand *(D5)*

Present-day, annotating the clerk's book in the margins, and now missing. Every
unverifiable reading in the game lives here, in the interpretation register
where it cannot be refuted. The modern hand can **point** — arrows, "see p.14",
"this does not match" — so the cord and chalk mechanics hang off annotations
and the Victorian never has to explain a twenty-first-century puzzle.

**The reveal must carry two disappearances** — 1918 and now — and they must
rhyme rather than repeat.
