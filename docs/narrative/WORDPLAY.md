# WORDPLAY — the San Greal shelf

A survey of reveals in the *san greal / sang real* class: wordplay that could
close a narrative like this one, ranked, with the back-story each one implies.
Research document, 18 Aug 2026. Nothing here is a decision.

---

## Why San Greal works — the anatomy

The Da Vinci Code's clincher is not that the pun is clever. It is that the pun
is **checkable and survives checking**: the misdivision of Old French
*san greal* (holy grail) into *sang real* (royal blood) is genuinely medieval —
it appears in fifteenth-century English writing (Hardyng's chronicle puns on
*sank royal*), centuries before Dan Brown. The reader who goes and looks finds
the author *right about the wordplay*, and that verified kernel launders the
invented everything-else.

So the anatomy, in the terms of this project:

> **The wordplay itself must be a T1.** The reading built on it may be as wild
> as you like — that is the T2/T3 layer — but the split, the anagram, the
> buried name must be real, attested, and findable in a minute. A reveal whose
> *mechanism* is invented dies at the debrief.

Four more properties, from taking San Greal apart:

1. **It re-reads something the audience already owns.** Grail legends were in
   everyone's head. The best candidates hide in words said daily.
2. **It lands aloud, in one sentence.** *San greal. Sang real.* No diagram.
3. **The two readings are causally linked**, not coincidentally similar — the
   split *is* the history. This is what separates it from a mere pun.
4. **It arrives last and reframes everything before it** — it is a turn, in
   this project's sense, for the whole book at once.

## Grading criteria

| | |
|---|---|
| **Snap** | Does it land aloud in one sentence, to a tired person in a pub? |
| **Truth** | T1 attested / hedged / contested / false. False is fatal unless the falseness is the point. |
| **Fresh** | Not burned by Dan Brown, QI, or every etymology listicle. |
| **Story** | Does a narrative fall out backwards — a keeper, a loss, a motive, a modern trace? |
| **Anchor** | Can a player stand in front of it? (Bonus: in London.) |

### D — Instrument fossils: the balance in the mind

All T1, verified 18 Aug 2026 (etymonline / Wiktionary / Merriam-Webster):

- **ponder** — *pondus*, a weight; to ponder is to weigh
- **deliberate** — *de + librare*, from **libra**, the scales; a deliberation is a weighing
- **examine** — *examen*, **the tongue of a balance**, the needle that shows the difference
- **scruple** — *scrupulus*, the small sharp pebble; *scrupulum*, the smallest apothecary weight (1/24 oz). A scruple is the least weight a conscience can register
- **grave / gravity** — *gravis*, heavy; a grave matter has weight
- **moment** — *momentum*, movement: in particular the small push that tips a beam — hence "of great moment" (sense-path hedged, endpoints T1)

Not one reveal — **six**, all pointing at the same buried instrument. Top five
as a cluster.

### E — Anagrams and letter games

| Candidate | The reveal | Truth | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **eleven plus two = twelve plus one** | Mutual anagrams; both equal thirteen; both are thirteen letters | T1 (arithmetic) | The only anagram in English that audits itself, three ways. Clincher for a clock-face narrative. |
| aptagrams: *astronomer/moon starer, the eyes/they see, a decimal point/I'm a dot in place, Statue of Liberty/built to stay free, listen/silent* | | T1 (mechanical) | Pretty, but see the caution below. |
| **honorificabilitudinitatibus** | Shakespeare's longest word, claimed by Baconians as the Latin anagram *hi ludi F. Baconis nati tuiti orbi* — "these plays, F. Bacon's offspring, are preserved for the world" | T1 that the claim was made; the claim itself is crankery | Not a reveal — a **cautionary tale**, and a gift: the Bacon-cipher literature is the Smyth of philology. |

**Caution on the whole class:** anagrams are the weakest genre for
"meant-to-be-there", because letter-shuffling visibly *permits* anything — the
Baconians proved it by finding whatever they wanted. An anagram clincher needs
an external audit (as *eleven plus two* has) or it reads as construction.

| Candidate | The reveal | Truth | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|

### The traps — famous, false, and therefore useful

*Sincere ≠ sine cera* ("without wax" — false, and Dan Brown already spent it),
*news* ≠ north-east-west-south, *posh* ≠ port-out-starboard-home, *Elephant &
Castle* ≠ *Infanta de Castilla*, *Charing Cross* ≠ *chère reine* (it is Old
English *cierring*, the river's turn), *Rotten Row* ≠ *Route du Roi*
(probably). All false; all beloved.

**The observation worth keeping:** folk etymology is to philology what the
pyramid inch is to metrology — the same desire for pattern, operating on real
objects, producing confident readings the record does not support. In a
Smyth-shaped narrative the false etymologies belong to the crank — and the
horror move is that some of his kind are **true** (tawdry, bonfire = bone-fire,
scapegoat all sound invented and are not). He was right that there was a
pattern. He was wrong about whose.

## The five

Ranked. Each with the reveal line, the true substrate, and the story you would
backwork from it.

### 1 · RULER — *"The ruler is the ruler."*

**The wordplay.** Not a pun: one word that never split. *Rule* and *ruler* are
Latin *regula*, "straight stick", from *regere*, "to keep straight, to direct,
to rule." *Rex* — king — is the same root. So are *right* (*rectus*: straight),
*regular*, *direct*, *correct*, *erect*, *rectitude*, *realm*, *royal*. And the
antonym seals it: **wrong** is Old Norse *rangr* — crooked, awry, kin to
*wring*. Right is straight. Wrong is twisted. The king is the keeper of the
straight stick, and English has been saying so in every moral sentence for a
thousand years. All T1, checkable in one minute.

**The backworked story.** Sovereignty *is* the monopoly on straightness. The
first standard yard is the king's own body — Henry I's arm, nose to
thumb-tip — because the ruler is the ruler, literally. Whoever holds the stick
holds the kingdom; a pretender's first act is to cut his own. Then one night in
1834 the King's palace burns and the stick burns inside it: the kingdom loses
its ruler in both senses at once, and the commission that rebuilds it is,
precisely, a regency. The clincher scene costs one prop: someone lays a
child's school ruler on a public standard and says the line.

**Why first.** Instant snap, perfect aloud (the two readings are *identical*
in sound because they are the same word), zero invention required, and the
political reading — measurement as sovereignty — powers an entire book, not
just its last page.

### 2 · CLEW — *"A clue is a ball of string."*

**The wordplay.** Old English *cliwen*, a ball of thread. Chaucer, telling
Ariadne's story c. 1385: Theseus escapes the labyrinth "by a clewe of twyn" —
a ball of twine, unwound behind him. The figurative sense — a thing that
guides you out of a perplexity — begins in the 1620s **explicitly as a
labyrinth metaphor**, and only later forgets the thread. Every detective
following clues is holding Ariadne's wool. T1; OED-solid.

**The backworked story.** A puzzle narrative in which the solvers carry a
length of cord — issued early, purpose unexplained — through a labyrinth of
streets, following clues. The last page reveals nothing new; it reveals the
word. They have been holding the clue since the beginning: not *a* clue,
**the** clue, the thousand-year-old object the word has meant all along. The
language remembered the labyrinth after everyone else forgot. As a final line:
*"You were never solving clues. You were holding one."*

**Why second.** The purest self-referential clincher on the list — the reveal
is about the machinery of the genre itself — and the only one where the
audience can be made to *hold the etymology in their hands* before they know
it. Slightly behind Ruler only because it needs the cord to be in hand; given
the cord, it may be first.

### 3 · MORTGAGE — *"Mort. Gage. The death-pledge."*

**The wordplay.** Old French *mort gage*, "dead pledge", carried into English
law by the Normans. The classical legal gloss (Littleton, then Coke) is that
the *pledge* dies — the land dies to the debtor on default, or the deal dies
on payment — but the death is in the deed either way, and forty million
English-speaking households recite the word monthly without hearing it. A
compound re-split rather than a one-letter shift, but aloud the effect is
exactly San Greal: a word you own, broken at the joint, with a corpse inside.
T1.

**The backworked story.** A City of London private bank, founded on the
wreckage of a dissolved military order, whose earliest ledger records its
loans in the old tongue: *gages mortz*. A modern protagonist refinancing a
flat finds their own surname on a fourteenth-century pledge roll — the family
has been paying the same death-pledge for six centuries, rolled over,
renamed, securitised. Clincher: *"You asked what the collateral was. Look at
the word. It was never the house."* The honest layer (the pledge dies) and
the sinister layer (the pledgor does) map perfectly onto fact vs reading.

**Why third.** The strongest *modern-thriller* candidate: maximal audience
ownership of the word, dead-simple snap, and the back-story arrives almost
without being asked. Loses to 1 and 2 only on freshness — lawyers know it,
and etymology listicles have found it, though no novel has spent it properly.

### 4 · SCAPEGOAT — *"Every scapegoat is sent to someone."*

**The wordplay.** Leviticus 16, the Day of Atonement: two goats, one "for the
LORD", one *la-Azazel* — for Azazel. Tyndale, 1530, unable to say what Azazel
was, read it as *ez ozel*, "the goat that departs", and coined **the (e)scape
goat**; the King James men kept it. Modern scholarship largely reads Azazel
as a name — the wilderness demon of the Book of Enoch, the fallen angel who
taught men weapons and cosmetics. So English's word for the innocent who
carries the blame was minted by **deleting an addressee**: the parcel is
still sent; the name on the label was translated away. Bonus, in the same
chapter: *atonement* — at-one-ment, the state of being made one — the other
great coinage of English theology. One chapter of Leviticus gave English both
its word for blame and its word for reconciliation, one hiding a demon, the
other hiding a doctrine, and both hide it by spelling. T1 throughout as
textual history.

**The backworked story.** A theological thriller. A 1610 committee note
surfaces showing the King James translators knew the word was a name and
buried it by vote. A modern congregation performs "scapegoat rituals" as
therapy — write the year's blame on a card, pin it to the straw goat, burn
it — unaware the ritual has an addressee and always did. Clincher: *"Tyndale
didn't mistranslate the word. He unaddressed it."*

**Why fourth.** Da-Vinci-adjacent in exactly the right way — a secret
produced by Bible translation, checkable in any concordance — and untouched
by Brown. Behind the top three because the snap needs two sentences of setup
(most audiences don't carry Leviticus 16), and the darkness narrows who wants
to read it.

### 5 · THE BALANCE IN THE MIND — *"You cannot think in English without weighing."*

**The wordplay.** Six everyday thinking-words, one buried instrument, all T1:
**ponder** (*pondus*, a weight), **deliberate** (*de librare* — from *libra*,
the scales: the £ sign is inside every deliberation), **examine** (*examen*,
the tongue of a balance — the needle itself), **scruple** (*scrupulum*, the
smallest apothecary weight: the least thing a conscience can register),
**grave** (*gravis*, heavy), **moment** (*momentum* — the push that tips the
beam; hence "of great moment"). English cannot describe careful thought
except as operations on a weighing instrument nobody has seen for centuries.

**The backworked story.** For a metrology narrative, the consolation and the
twist at once. The standards were burned, melted, dispersed — and it never
mattered, because the instrument had already emigrated into the verbs, where
no fire reaches. A dying metrologist's last letter: *"They melted the bars.
But every jury that weighs the evidence, every scrupulous clerk, every
deliberation in every parliament still swings on the beam. The balance got
into the language, and the language is the only standard that was never
lost."* Courtroom clincher, one word: *"Members of the jury — deliberate.
The word is the instruction: bring out the scales."*

**Why fifth.** The slowest snap — it is a cluster, not a split, and needs
thirty seconds rather than three — but the compound interest is the largest
on the list, and it is the freshest: the set has never been assembled in
fiction. For a book (rather than a film-style single reveal) it may
outperform everything above it, because it can be *dealt out* across
chapters, one word per turn, and collected at the end.

## Fedora fit (separable from the general ranking)

The book already ends on a withheld loop (Southampton is a coincidence; the
researcher is never found; nobody comes). A San Greal-class reveal slots in
without breaking any locked decision, because wordplay is a **reading of the
language** — unrefutable by construction, exactly where the red-team said
fiction must live, and yet built on T1s a player can check and find true.

Candidate placements, no decisions implied:

| Candidate | Where it lands |
|---|---|
| **Clew** | The cord. The players carry a marked string through London following crossword clues; the debrief tells them what the word *clue* has meant for a thousand years. Retroactively reframes the entire mechanic — the project's favourite move, applied to itself. Costs nothing; the prop already exists. |
| **Ruler** | Chapter 21's thesis-sentence. The whole spine — the king's arm, the burned yard, the regency of the commission, right/straight, wrong/crooked, the state measuring men — compresses into *"the ruler is the ruler"*. Unlike Southampton, a coincidence that isn't one. |
| **Nowhere = now here** | D22, the researcher. A last margin note reading "I am nowhere" that the narrator — or a player — eventually re-splits. One letter's move turns an absence into a location, which is precisely what (b)-variant D11 does physically. If D11(b) is chosen, this is its epigraph. Pure mechanism, no historical substrate: honest to grade as a pun, and as a pun about disappearance it is the best in English. |
| **The Exchequer cluster** | Chapter 2. The chessboard-office, *shah mat*, the counterfoil in every chequebook, the tally fire as a checkmate delivered to William IV. The check-chain is T1; keep "bank stock from tally stock" out or hedged — it is contested. |
| **Meantime** | Chapter 9, the fortnight. *"Britain paid in September and collected in October, and everyone since has lived in the mean time."* A pun, graded honestly, placed as a reading. |
| **Balance cluster** | Chapter 21's consolation, after the debrief separates fact from fiction: measurement moved out of bodies and into paper — but it had already moved into the language, where it cannot be lost. Answers the book's own thesis. |
| **Noon moved** | Texture for 9 or 14: even midday has drifted; nothing holds still, including the words for stillness. |

**One warning, from the anatomy.** These reveals are strongest one at a time.
San Greal works because it is alone at the summit of its book; five stacked
clinchers would read as a listicle. If the book takes any of this, it takes
**one** as the summit (Clew or Ruler), one as an epigraph-scale grace note
(Now Here), and leaves the rest as connective tissue or nothing.
