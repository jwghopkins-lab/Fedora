# GROUND TRUTH — ten linking stories, ranked

What single story ties the lost bars, the lost chairs and the statue together.
Eleven candidates generated, two rejected outright, nine served in order.

---

## First: two research findings that change the ground

### The bars are numbered, and some are found

I have been saying *no register exists*. That is too strong and it must be
corrected.

- **Parliamentary copy No. 34** is in the **Museums Victoria** collection,
  Melbourne.
- **Bronze Yard No. 11** went to the **United States**.

So the forty bars carry **numbers**, several are traceable, and what is missing
is not a register but a *consolidated* one. That is far better material than an
undifferentiated fog: a numbered series with gaps lets you ask about **one
specific number** and get silence.

### The restored yard descends from three bars that were somewhere else

The 1845 standard was constructed from three surviving comparisons:

- **A1** and **A2**, both made for the **Ordnance Survey**
- **R.S. 46**, the yard of the **Royal Astronomical Society**

All three had been compared against the Imperial standard *before* the fire.

**Two of the three parents of the national yard belonged to the Ordnance
Survey** — the organisation Clarke would spend twenty-seven years working for at
Southampton, comparing the world's standards against each other.

That is a causal link I did not have, and it needs corroborating against Airy's
own account, but if it holds it is the strongest join in the whole story.

# The nine, in order

## 1 · The custodian chain
**People keep things they were told to give up. That is the only reason anything
survives.**

- **Rivett**, 1649: ordered by Parliament to break up the statue. Hides it,
  fakes the evidence, keeps it eleven years.
- **Beatson**, 1838: breaks up the Temeraire for profit and has her timbers made
  into church furniture and twelve chairs.
- **The clerk**, 1900: nobody comes for the parish brass, so he keeps it.
- **The researcher**, now: posts the book to a stranger rather than let it go
  back in a cupboard.

Four people, three of them real, none of them acting on instruction. **Nothing
in this story is preserved by an institution. Everything that survives was kept
by somebody who was not supposed to keep it.**

*Why it ranks first:* it is already half-built — D16 is exactly this — it needs
no invention, it explains the object in the players' hands, and it is the only
candidate that makes the modern half moving rather than merely clever. It also
sits perfectly with D18: nobody is protecting anything, and things survive
anyway, by accident and stubbornness.

*Risk:* it is a theme rather than a plot. Needs one concrete object moving
through all four hands to become a story.

## 2 · Three survivors
**The nation's length was recovered from three bars that happened not to be in
the room, and two of them belonged to the surveyors.**

Everything after 1834 rests on A1, A2 and R.S. 46. Not on the destroyed
original — on three working copies that were out of the building on the night.
And the two Ordnance Survey bars lead directly to Southampton and to Clarke.

*Why it ranks second:* it converts a coincidence into a chain. The restoration,
the Ordnance Survey, Clarke's comparisons and the figure of the Earth stop being
adjacent facts and become one lineage. It also gives the fiction a beautifully
narrow place to live — **not in whether the three were used, but in whether
anyone ever went back and checked them against each other afterwards.**

*Risk:* needs corroborating. If A1 and A2 turn out not to be Ordnance Survey
bars the idea loses most of its force.

## 3 · Counterfeit provenance
**A trade in objects claiming to be the thing they commemorate — running
alongside a system where being the real object is the entire point.**

- Rivett sold brass-handled cutlery to both sides, claiming it was the statue,
  while the statue sat hidden on his premises. **Documented.**
- Rotherhithe was a shipbreaking town full of old ship's oak, and the Temeraire
  relic trade ran through it. Provenance on relic timber is notoriously soft.
- Meanwhile, forty bars exist for whom authenticity is not sentimental but
  absolute: a bar either is the standard or is worthless.

*Why it ranks third:* two documented real cases, and it is the theme stated as
plot. The whole book asks whether a thing is what it claims to be.

*Risk:* it points at fakery, and our establishment is guilty of inattention, not
forgery. Keep it in the object world, away from the institutions.

## 4 · Nothing is the original of itself
**Every object on the walk is a replacement presented as the thing.**

- The statue's **sword, buckles and straps fell off in 1810**. The **sword and
  the George were stolen in 1844**, during a visit by Queen Victoria. What is up
  there now includes parts that are not Le Sueur's.
- The Trafalgar plates may have been **re-cut in 1952** — a source says so
  plainly, and it needs corroborating.
- The yard itself is a replacement for a burnt original, reconstructed by
  inference.
- The Eleanor Cross at the station is a **replica, 222 yards from the site**.

*Why it ranks fourth:* it is the most purely on-theme of all of them, and every
instance is real. It also reframes the walk: a tour of replacements that nobody
has ever announced as replacements.

*Risk:* it is a catalogue rather than a narrative. Better as the *argument* of
the book than its plot.

## 5 · The numbered series
**Forty numbered objects. Two findable. Ask about a third and the record stops.**

No. 11 is in America. No. 34 is in Melbourne. The rest are a numbering with no
consolidated list behind it.

*Why it ranks fifth:* now that we know they carry numbers, this becomes a
puzzle-shaped thing rather than a vague absence — and a specific number is
something the diary can name, the cord can spell, and a search engine cannot
resolve. Folds in the idea of **a number that exists in the sequence and not in
the world**.

*Risk:* the moment somebody finds a consolidated list, it collapses. Check hard
before building on it.

## 6 · The freemasonry of people who check
**The only people who could have found the error had no standing to report it.**

Inspectors, vestry clerks, surveyors' assistants — a network of people whose job
was comparison, who knew each other by post, and none of whom could publish.
Smyth's letter survey runs straight through them.

*Why it ranks sixth:* it answers the sharpest objection anyone has raised — *how
would a parish clerk ever know?* He would not, alone. He is a node in a survey.

*Risk:* structural rather than dramatic. It is scaffolding for the plot, not the
plot.

## 7 · The inspector's hand
**Knowledge that lived in a body and had no successor.**

A man who compared the same parish yards for thirty years and knew by hand which
felt short. He dies and it goes with him — like the boys thrown into nettles so
a boundary would survive in court, except nobody did the equivalent for the
standards.

*Why it ranks seventh:* it is the second instance of memory-as-evidence, and it
works by *contrast* with the nettles rather than repetition. A boundary is a
place and a body can hold a place. **A length cannot be held in a body**, and
that is the point.

*Risk:* wholly invented, and it wants to be a scene rather than a spine.

## 8 · Relics and references
**Two kinds of object, and a moment when somebody confused them.**

A chair made of Temeraire oak has value by association: it does not matter if it
is a millimetre out. A bronze bar has value only by being exactly right. Both
are wooden and metal things in cupboards, and both get moved by people who
cannot tell which kind they are handling.

*Why it ranks eighth:* the cleanest statement of what the book is actually
about. It also explains, without any villain, how a thing of absolute precision
ends up treated as a keepsake and lost.

*Risk:* abstract. Hard to hang on a specific object without inventing one.

## 9 · Three moves, no inventory
**1900, 1938, 1955.** The vestries dissolved. The Standards Department shaken
out of the Jewel Tower. St Paul's Rotherhithe demolished and its Temeraire
furniture carried to St Mary's.

*Why it ranks last of those kept:* it is a genuine and slightly chilling
pattern, and all three are real. But it is a rhythm, not a story — best used as
the structure the plot moves through rather than the plot itself.

# Rejected, with reasons

**Turner as the single witness.** He watched the Palace burn in 1834 and painted
the Temeraire in 1839, so both objects pass through one man's sight. **Rejected:
it is a coincidence, not a causation.** Turner connected nothing; he was in two
places. Building a spine on it would require inventing an intention he
demonstrably did not have, and his sketchbooks are digitised and would refute it.
Keep him as the eye of the story, not its engine.

**The cord as the story.** An object whose only purpose is to check other
objects, passed hand to hand, never itself checked. **Rejected: that is the
mechanic, not the narrative.** Promoting a prop to a protagonist would make the
invented thing load-bearing, which is the one rule we have never broken.

---

# The recommendation

**Ranks 1 and 2 are not competitors — they are the two halves.**

**Three survivors** is the historical spine: the lineage runs from three bars
that were out of the building, through the Ordnance Survey, to Clarke at
Southampton, to the figure of the Earth. All causal, nearly all documented.

**The custodian chain** is the human one: Rivett, Beatson, the clerk, the
researcher. Four people who kept something they were not supposed to keep, and
the only reason anything reached the players' hands at all.

Run them together and every loose object gets a job. The bars have a lineage.
The chairs and the statue have custodians. And the thing in the players' hands
is the fourth link in a chain that started in 1649 with a metalsmith lying to
Parliament.
