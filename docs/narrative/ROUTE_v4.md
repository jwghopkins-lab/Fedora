## FILL 1 · Charing Cross — the datum point of the country

**The single best fill available, and it is pure measurement.**

The equestrian statue of Charles I has stood since 1675 on the site of the last
and most elaborate of the Eleanor crosses, built 1291–94 by Edward I and pulled
down in 1647. **A plaque beside the statue states that mileages from London are
measured from the site of the original Cross.**

Every road sign in Britain that says how far it is to London is counting from
that spot. It is the origin of the national road network — a measurement datum,
standing in the open, that a hundred thousand people walk past every day without
knowing they are at the zero point.

It sits at the top of Whitehall, at the south side of Trafalgar Square. It fills
the dead leg exactly, needs no detour, and it is on theme in a way nothing we cut
ever was.

## FILL 2 · The Temeraire is wrong, and nobody noticed for thirty-eight years

**Turner needs no invention. The oddity is documented.**

- **Her masts were already gone.** Navy Board records show the upper masts were
  removed at Sheerness in July 1838 and the lower masts the month after, before
  the tow. Turner painted her fully rigged and riding high.
- **The sun is in the wrong place.** She is being towed *west*, up the Thames
  from Sheerness, so the setting sun should be ahead of her. Turner put it
  behind.
- **There were two tugs, not one.**
- **There was a full moon that night**, not the new moon he painted.

And the fact that does the work: **nobody remarked on the sun until James
Dafforne pointed it out in 1877** — thirty-eight years after it was painted, and
by then it was already one of the best-loved pictures in the country.

That is the Nelson's Column pattern exactly, in the National Gallery, at a part
we already have. A celebrated national object with an obvious error in it that
went unremarked for decades because everybody looked and nobody checked.

**It also replaces what the lions and the Cenotaph were doing** — the thesis
stated in an object — so cutting those costs the argument less than it looked.

## Voltaire, corrected and promoted

**He was not stabbed.** He was **beaten**, in Paris in 1726, by servants of the
Chevalier de Rohan after answering an insult. He was briefly held in the
Bastille and released on condition he leave the country.

**That is why he was in London at all.** No invention needed, and it makes him
the third beating in the story:

- Boys beaten at boundary marks so that a measurement would survive.
- Voltaire beaten out of France for insolence to a man above him.
- Smyth's reputation beaten out of the Royal Society for the same offence in
  different clothes.

Terriss and the stage door should come out of the clue — it muddies the beat.
