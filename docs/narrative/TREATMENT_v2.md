## The premise

In October 1834 the Palace of Westminster burned down. The fire had been
started deliberately, in a stove, by clerks disposing of two cart-loads of
Exchequer tally sticks — notched willow rods, an obsolete way of keeping
accounts, out of use since 1826. The flue overheated. By morning most of the
Palace was gone.

Among the things that burned was the imperial standard yard. Not a copy. The
bar that defined how long a yard was.

A painter took a boat out onto the river to watch. He had not brought colours,
only a sketchbook, and by the time he was level with the bridge he had stopped
trying to draw the building at all. What he drew was the light on the water. He
filled two books that night and painted it twice afterwards, and he had been
born four hundred yards from where this story ends.

It took twenty years to work out how long a yard had been. A commission was
appointed in 1838 under George Biddell Airy, the Astronomer Royal, and the
restored standard did not become law until 1855. The method was comparison:
they gathered every surviving copy they could find, measured them against each
other, and reasoned back to the thing that no longer existed.

They did it very well. They did not do it perfectly, and there is no possible
world in which they could have.

That is the whole story. Everything else follows from it.

---

## What the fire cost

Forty bronze bars were made by Troughton and Simms. One became the new Imperial
Standard. Four were Parliamentary Copies, placed apart on purpose so that no
single fire could ever do this again: one at the Royal Mint, one at the Royal
Society, one at the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, and one walled into the
masonry of the new Palace at Westminster.

The other thirty-five went out to, in the phrase every account uses, the cities
of London, Edinburgh and Dublin, the United States and other countries.

There is no register of them. Every source stops at that sentence.

In 1876 three public standards were set into stone where anyone could reach
them — at the Guildhall, at the gate of the Observatory at Greenwich, and into
the north terrace of Trafalgar Square. Brass markers giving the foot, the yard,
the perch, the chain, and a hundred feet in ten-foot steps, with the working
temperature cut into the stone: at 62 degrees Fahrenheit. They were put
outdoors deliberately, so that any citizen could kneel down and check that a
tradesman was not cheating them.

They are still there. They were lifted and reset a few metres east in 2003, and
no source states whether the plates now in the ground are the 1876 originals or
recuts.

## Southampton

At the Ordnance Survey office in Southampton, a Royal Engineer called Alexander
Ross Clarke spent twenty-seven years comparing standards of length. He
published the comparisons in 1866: England, France, Belgium, Prussia, Russia,
India, Australia, all measured against one another to a precision that other
geodesists still describe as a model of the work.

In 1887 the Royal Society gave him its Royal Medal. The citation was for his
comparison of standards of length, and his determination of the figure of the
earth.

Those were not two achievements. They were one job. In Victorian geodesy the
shape of the planet was computed out of the length standards, because there was
nothing else to compute it from. Clarke's ellipsoids — Clarke 1866 and Clarke
1880 — were the results.

Clarke 1866 became the basis of the North American Datum of 1927. It is still
the datum of record for older railways, irrigation canals and pipelines across
North America, kept deliberately because re-surveying them would cost more than
living with it. It is in every geographic information system in the world.
Clarke 1880 underpins several African and French datums that are also still in
use.

So this is not a question about a museum object. Whatever was in those brass
bars is in the ground, under infrastructure, today.

## The crank

Charles Piazzi Smyth was the Astronomer Royal for Scotland. He was a serious
observer — he dragged a telescope up a mountain in Tenerife to demonstrate that
altitude improves seeing, and he was right, and mountaintop observatories are
what happened next.

In 1864 he published a book arguing that the Great Pyramid encoded a unit of
length of divine origin, that the British inch descended from it, and that the
restored yard was therefore wrong by about a thousandth.

He believed this. He believed it for reasons that were nonsense.

In 1874 the Royal Society rejected his paper on the design of the pyramid. It
was right to. Anyone can read what the referees read. In 1888, after Airy
publicly savaged the state of his observatory, Smyth resigned his Fellowship of
the Royal Society and left the Royal Observatory in the same year. He died at
Ripon in February 1900, aged eighty-one, twelve years out of the argument, and
he is remembered — where he is remembered at all — as the man who went strange
about pyramids.

He said two things. He said the yard was wrong. He said the yard was wrong
because of divine revelation.

Only one of those was ever tested.

## What Newton wrote

The obvious response to Smyth is that only a fool goes looking for units of
measurement in a pyramid.

In 1646 John Greaves, the Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford, published
a survey of the pyramids at Giza. Isaac Newton owned a copy. Working from
Greaves's measurements, Newton wrote a dissertation on the sacred cubit of the
Jews, published in 1737 — he wanted the Egyptian cubit in order to recover the
Hebrew one, in order to reconstruct the Temple of Solomon for his work on
chronology and prophecy.

The pyramid inch that Smyth built his life on is one twenty-fifth of the cubit
Newton postulated.

So in 1874 the Royal Society rejected a paper about measurement in the Great
Pyramid while holding a manuscript on measurement in the Great Pyramid written
by Isaac Newton.

Newton wrote it in a house off Leicester Square, where he lived from 1710. The
house was pulled down in 1913. There is a public library on the site now, and a
plaque inside it at the top of the stairs.

This does not make Smyth right. It makes the contempt unsafe.

## A fortnight in 1884

Britain had spent decades resisting the metric system. There was a lobby
devoted to it: the International Institute for Preserving and Perfecting
Anglo-Saxon Weights and Measures, of which Smyth was a vice-president, which
published a magazine and had a song.

On 17 September 1884, the British government signed the Metre Convention.

On 1 October 1884, the International Meridian Conference opened in Washington.
On the 13th it resolved that the meridian passing through the transit
instrument at Greenwich would be the prime meridian of the world. The vote was
twenty-two to one.

The transit instrument in question was Airy's. He had built it, and it had
taken its first observation in 1851. The same man had chaired the commission
that rebuilt the yard.

Two weeks between the signature and the meridian. Britain paid in September and
collected in October. The two events sit in the open record, and nobody lines
them up, because every history ever written files them under different
subjects.

And then Britain did nothing. A Select Committee recommended in 1895 that
metric measure be made compulsory within two years. The Act of 1897 legalised
it and did not compel it. That signature sat untouched for eighty years, until
a minister stood up in the Commons in May 1965 and said British industry would
convert by 1975.

## 1881

Three years before that fortnight, Clarke was told he was to be posted
overseas. He had been at the same table in Southampton for twenty-seven years.

He resigned instead.

It is on the record and it has never been explained.

He outlived all of them. Airy died in 1892, Smyth in 1900, Strachey — who sat
for Britain at the meridian conference — in 1908. Clarke died in 1914, and
published nothing further on the subject.

## Beating the bounds

Before there were maps anyone could rely on, a parish established its boundary
by walking it. Once a year, in the week Ascension Day falls in, the clergy and
the parish went round the line and struck the marks — stones, gateposts, walls,
trees — with willow wands.

They took the boys, and at each mark they hurt them. Beaten, or bumped against
the stone, or thrown into nettles, or ducked.

This was not cruelty for its own sake. The perambulation was the legal record.
There was no other. A boundary was whatever the parish could collectively
remember, and memory had to hold up in court, so it was installed in bodies
that would still be alive in sixty years.

It worked. A man of seventy-five once won a boundary case for his parish by
testifying that he knew exactly where the eastern line ran, because he had been
thrown into a heap of nettles on it when he was a boy. Saying he remembered
would not have been enough. The nettles were the evidence.

The Tithe Commutation Act of 1836 produced the tithe maps. From 1862 the
Ordnance Survey published definitive maps at twenty-five inches and six inches
to the mile, and the walking stopped.

Where a boundary was disputed, the Ordnance Survey's surveyors asked the
memories of men who had been beaten as children.

Saint Martin-in-the-Fields still beats its bounds.

## Light

The other problem the Victorians had was getting light into places that had
none.

Under the pavements of every commercial street in London there were vaults, and
the vaults had no windows. So they set thick glass into the flagstones — iron
frames holding lenses cut to bend daylight sideways, so that a cellar with no
opening to the sky could be worked in. Hayward Brothers of Union Street in
Borough patented a prismatic version in 1871 and made more of them than anyone.
Thousands survive, and almost nobody walking over them looks down.

Above ground it was gas, piped under the footway and lit by hand, every evening,
by a man with a pole. A few of those lamps are still burning in courts off Saint
Martin's Lane.

The electric light arrived on the Victoria Embankment in 1878 and in the Savoy
Theatre in October 1881. Within a generation both solutions were obsolete, and
the objects that solved the problem stayed exactly where they were, doing
nothing, in the ground.

None of this has anything to do with standards of length. That comes later.

## The century after

The Standard Weights and Measures Department worked out of the Jewel Tower in
Old Palace Yard — one of the two buildings that had survived the 1834 fire —
from 1869 until 1938. It left because road traffic had grown to the point where
the vibration made precise measurement impossible. The city shook the standards
out of their own building. Airy's transit circle went out of service the same
year.

In 1959 the yard was redefined as exactly 0.9144 metres, by agreement. From
that moment the bar in the case was a museum object. The definition no longer
came from anything you could hold.

In May 1965 the President of the Board of Trade told the Commons that British
industry would convert to metric, sector by sector, and finish by 1975. In 1967
Parliament passed the Decimal Currency Act, and the sixpence began walking
towards the exit.

That same year, on the second of April, a bronze bust of Admiral Cunningham was
unveiled on the north terrace of Trafalgar Square, twenty paces from the public
standards. It was made by Franta Belsky, who had arrived in Britain from Brno in
1939 with nothing and spent the war in the Czechoslovak Brigade.

Sealed inside the bust are a half-pint bottle of Guinness, a copy of that day's
newspaper, a signed note, and a sixpence.

He did that to every casting he made.

On 21 October 1983, in Paris, the General Conference on Weights and Measures
voted to stop defining the metre as an object and to define it instead as the
distance light travels in a vacuum in one two-hundred-and-ninety-nine-million,
seven-hundred-and-ninety-two-thousand, four-hundred-and-fifty-eighth of a
second.

The unit stopped being a thing and became a property of light.

Twelve days later a statue of Lord Mountbatten was unveiled off Horse Guards
Road. Belsky made it. Hidden in the left leg is a jam jar containing coins,
press cuttings and the details of his commission. It was cast by a foundry in
Peckham called Meridian.

A man who left his own name inside every bronze he made was doing something
more than signing it. A signature nobody can ever read is not a signature.

## 1900

The London Government Act abolished the vestries. Twenty-eight new metropolitan
boroughs absorbed the property of dozens of parishes in a single movement.
Among that property were the local standards — each parish's own yard and its
own set of weights, against which an officer had gone out along the shops to
check that nobody was being cheated.

Smyth died in February of that year.

At Saint Martin-in-the-Fields the parish's brass was in a case in a back room,
and nobody came for it. The clerk wrote twice to ask what should be done with
it, and got no reply.

So he kept it. Keeping it required nothing.

And having kept it, he eventually read what was in the drawer beside it: a run
of comparison certificates going back decades, and a bundle of letters from a
Scottish astronomer who in the 1880s had been writing to vestry clerks up and
down the country, by post, at his own expense, asking what their local yard
measured against the imperial and when it had last been compared. He could not
get official figures. So he was collecting his own.

Careful, patient, useful fieldwork, done for a reason that was nonsense, by a
man nobody would collate.

The clerk died in the influenza in 1918. Nobody took a statement. That autumn,
nobody was taking statements about anything.

## The present

His survey book was never in an archive, because it was never officially a
document. A parish officer's working record, in a cupboard.

Everything else went online. Newton's manuscript. Airy's account of the
construction of the new standard. Clarke's comparisons. The proceedings of the
meridian conference. The vestry records. Five collections that had never spoken
to one another, all searchable, none of it hidden, and none of it assembled
because nobody had ever had a reason to put those five things on the same desk.

Somebody did. They worked through the clerk's book, annotated the margins in
pencil, went out at odd hours and measured things, and then stopped in the
middle of a sentence.

Before they stopped they put the book in the post to someone they had never
met.

## What the ending is

The narrator reads two hands — a parish clerk and whoever wrote in the margins
— and works out that the marginalia is not commentary. It is instructions.

They walk it. Westminster, Whitehall, Trafalgar Square, the church, Covent
Garden. A length of marked cord against a series of real objects, one letter at
a time. The letters make a word.

The word is SOUTHAMPTON.

They look up. Southampton Street runs from the Strand to the Covent Garden
piazza and crosses Maiden Lane, where Turner was born, four hundred yards from
where the walk ends.

Southampton is where Clarke spent twenty-seven years comparing the standards
that the shape of the Earth was computed from.

It is a coincidence. Southampton Street is named for the Earls of Southampton,
through the Bedford estate, and has nothing whatever to do with the Ordnance
Survey.

Nobody is following the narrator. Nobody ever was. There is no institution
protecting anything, because nothing needs protecting: the yard has been
defined as exactly 0.9144 metres since 1959, and no one alive is embarrassed by
a bar of brass.

They look for the person who sent the book, and they find almost everything.
Where they lived. What they read, and the order they read it in. The week they
stopped. Not the person.

By then the reader has spent twenty chapters waiting for someone to come, and
should be able to feel the exact moment they started expecting it.

## The three people who are not real

**The clerk.** There was a vestry at Saint Martin-in-the-Fields, it held local
standards, and they went to the borough in 1900. The man who kept them is
invented.

**The researcher.** Invented.

**The cord.** Invented.

Everything else in this document happened, and can be checked in about an hour.

## What changed, and what it costs the walk

**Turner is now in the first section**, not only at the end. He is the bridge
between the two threads — the man who spent his life on light, on a boat,
watching the standard burn — and the first version buried him.

**Light has its own section**, placed before the century after, so that the
1983 redefinition is a payoff and not a curiosity. Parts 20, 21 and 23 of the
walk now serve something.

**The century after is new.** It carries the Jewel Tower and the vibration, the
1959 redefinition, 1965, 1967 and 1983, and it gives Belsky the place the first
version denied him. Parts 4, 15 and 16 now serve something.

**Newton's London address is named** but the library is not, so the walk can
still make finding it the work.

**One thing this document may do and the walk must not.** The book names
Southampton in its own section heading, long before the ending. The walk cannot.
On the terrace at part 12 the explainer should say that the shape of the Earth
was computed from bars like these, by a Royal Engineer who spent twenty-seven
years at it **in a town on the south coast** — and stop there. Then the word at
the end is recognition rather than information.
