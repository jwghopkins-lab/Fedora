# GROUND TRUTH — the big invention

Three narratives for a royal-substitution story, built on the measurement
material. Five were generated; three survive. Every claim is tagged **T1**
(verifiable), **T2** (a reading, unfalsifiable), **T3** (invented).

---

## The principle that should govern this

**Fork the line at a place that is already permanently disputed.** Then our
fiction adds nothing falsifiable, because the ground was never settled and
never can be.

Two candidates qualify and both are mainstream scholarship, not fringe:

**1442 · Edward IV** — T1. The claim that Edward IV was fathered by an archer
while Richard, Duke of York was at Pontoise. **Michael Jones found a document in
Rouen Cathedral indicating Richard and Cecily were about a hundred miles apart
during the five weeks in which conception must have fallen.** Channel 4 broadcast
*Britain's Real Monarch* on this premise in 2004, presented by Tony Robinson,
tracing the alternative line to **the 14th Earl of Loudoun, a farmer in
Jerilderie, New South Wales**, who died in 2012. Historians have challenged it.
Nobody has closed it.

**1688 · the warming pan** — T1, and **the better fork for us**, because it lands
inside our timeline. James II's son was alleged to have been smuggled into the
birthing chamber in a warming pan.

And here is the detail that makes it ours: **James II commissioned an enquiry
into his own son's legitimacy, took depositions from over seventy witnesses, and
published them. Nobody believed him.**

That is our thesis running backwards. Nelson's Column was wrong for a century
and a half because nobody checked. The warming-pan baby was disbelieved *even
though everybody checked*. **Evidence failed in both directions, and the failure
is documented both times.**

---

## The fact that makes all three narratives work

**Isaac Newton was Warden of the Mint from 1696 and Master from 1699 until his
death in 1727.** All T1:

- He ran the Great Recoinage and estimated **twenty per cent of the coin taken in
  was counterfeit**.
- He went after counterfeiters personally: undercover in taverns, more than
  fifty-eight interviews in Newgate, **over a hundred cross-examinations between
  June 1698 and December 1699**.
- He put **William Chaloner** on the gallows for high treason in 1699.
- And he wrote **A Dissertation upon the Sacred Cubit of the Jews**.

So, in one sentence, entirely true:

> **Newton spent thirty-one years hanging men for passing base metal as royal,
> under a dynasty installed on a disputed birth — while privately trying to
> recover a standard of length that predated kings altogether.**

And it rhymes backwards, T1 both ends:

> **John Rivett committed in 1649 exactly the crime Newton prosecuted in 1699 —
> selling base metal as the king — and got away with it because the king he
> counterfeited was already dead.**

# NARRATIVE A · The King's Arm

**The strongest. Recommended.**

### The premise

A unit was a body. The yard is traditionally the distance from the nose to the
thumb of **Henry I** — *tradition, and probably apocryphal: flag it as legend,
never as fact.* The ell, the foot, the fathom, the span: every English measure of
length was originally a part of a person, and the authoritative person was the
king.

**So the authority of the measure and the authority of the crown are the same
authority.** If the body is substituted, the measure is void.

### The shape

| | | type |
|---|---|---|
| **1649** | Charles I executed. Parliament orders his statue broken up. Rivett hides it, fakes the evidence, and sells brass cutlery claiming it is the king — to both sides | **T1** |
| **1675** | The statue is re-erected at Charing Cross, which becomes the point the country measures from | **T1** |
| **1688** | A child is alleged to be substituted. Seventy depositions are published. Nobody believes them | **T1** |
| **1696–1727** | Newton at the Mint, hunting false metal, writing on the sacred cubit | **T1** |
| **1701** | The Act of Settlement passes over some fifty nearer claimants to reach Hanover | **T1** |
| | Newton concluded that a standard resting on a contested body was no standard, and looked for one that needed no king | **T2** |
| **1834** | The yard burns | **T1** |
| **1845** | It is rebuilt from three bars that were out of the building — two of them the Ordnance Survey's | **T1** |
| | By then nobody alive remembers the unit was ever a body | **T2** |
| **1884** | Britain signs the metre away on 17 September and takes the meridian on 1 October | **T1** |
| | The keepers are a chain, not a society: Newton's papers, then Turner, then Smyth, then a parish clerk | **T3 — the chain only** |

### Why it is the best of the three

- **The fork is unfalsifiable by construction.** Three hundred and thirty-seven
  years of dispute, seventy published depositions, and the argument still runs.
- **Every hinge is a real object we already have**: the statue, the datum, the
  bars, the terrace.
- **Newton is not shoehorned in.** He is genuinely the joint — the man
  responsible for whether English metal was what it claimed to be, who privately
  wanted a measure older than kings.
- **The living alternative line makes no claim.** The Jacobite heir today is
  **Franz, Duke of Bavaria** — T1 — who is not a British subject, has never
  asserted anything, and would presumably be appalled. **That is precisely why
  it is safe**: nobody is accused of anything, and the story is elegiac rather
  than accusatory.

### What is invented

Only the **chain of keepers**, and the reading that Newton's cubit work had a
political motive. **No living person does anything. No named historical person
commits a crime they are not already recorded as committing.**

# NARRATIVE B · What Newton Was Looking For

**The subtler version. Keeps the royal fork, moves the emphasis.**

### The premise

The conspiracy is not about who should be on the throne. It is about what Newton
concluded while he was in charge of the nation's metal.

He had access no one else had: the coin standards, the trial plates, the Trial
of the Pyx. **T1** — the Pyx is a real, continuous ceremony in which the coinage
is tested against standard plates, and it has run since the twelfth century.

**T2:** a man with those instruments, that arithmetic, and thirty-one years is
the one person in England who could have noticed the standards drifting.

**T3:** he did. And he understood that the drift did not matter arithmetically —
it mattered *constitutionally*, because a standard whose authority descends from
a crown is only as sound as the crown. He had watched that crown change hands in
1688 on a disputed birth, and again in 1701 by statute over fifty nearer heirs.

So he went looking for a measure that owed nothing to any king: the cubit of the
Temple, recovered from Greaves's survey of Giza.

### Why it ranks second

It gives Newton a **motive** rather than a coincidence, and it is the most
intellectually satisfying of the three. But the royal element becomes background
— which is exactly what was asked to be foregrounded. **Best used as Narrative
A's engine room rather than as an alternative.**

# NARRATIVE C · The Man on the Horse

**The most physical, and the one the walk can actually test.**

### The premise

The statue is the falsified object, and unlike a bloodline **a statue can be
measured**.

- **T1**: Le Sueur cast it about 1633. Rivett was ordered to destroy it, hid it,
  produced broken brass as proof of compliance, and sold cutlery he claimed was
  made from it.
- **T1**: it was re-erected in 1675 on a pedestal Wren designed — **and sources
  disagree about who carved it**, Grinling Gibbons or Joshua Marshall.
- **T1**: **the sword, buckles and straps fell off in 1810. The sword and the
  George were stolen in 1844, during a visit by Queen Victoria.** What stands
  there now is not all Le Sueur's.
- **T2**: an object that has shed and regrown its parts for four centuries, at
  the exact point the country measures from, is a strange thing to have made the
  origin of the national road network.
- **T3**: Rivett took a mould before he hid it. What went up in 1675 is the cast,
  and the original is where he put it.

### Why it ranks third, and why it is still valuable

**As a standalone conspiracy it is thin** — it is about a statue, not a country,
and "the statue is a copy" does not by itself threaten anything.

**But it is the only one of the three the players can physically interrogate.**
Le Sueur's recorded dimensions exist. The cord can be laid against the pedestal.
The parts that are demonstrably replacements are documented with dates.

**So: fold it into A as the proof step.** The bloodline is the claim; the statue
is the thing you can put a string against.

# Recommendation

**Run A, powered by B, proved by C.**

- **A** gives the reveal: the measure was a body, the body was substituted, and
  everything measured since descends from a disputed authority.
- **B** gives Newton his motive and the story its brain.
- **C** gives the walk something to measure, which is the only reason any of it
  reaches a pavement.

**And the whole thing stays honest**, because the two loads it carries are:
a three-hundred-year-old dispute nobody has ever closed, and a chain of keepers
who are invented and who never touch a verifiable fact — Rivett, Newton, Turner,
Smyth and a parish clerk, four of them real and doing only what the record
already says they did.

## If you fork this away from the measurement story

Everything above stands alone. The measurement material — Clarke, Airy, the
terrace, the meridian fortnight — can stay with the quieter book, and **The
King's Arm** can become its own thing. The only shared object is the statue at
Charing Cross, and it is large enough to appear in two stories without either
noticing.
