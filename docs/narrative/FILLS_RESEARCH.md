# GROUND TRUTH — deep research on the two fills

Charing Cross and the Temeraire, worked to the bottom. Organised as asked:
**leads**, **gaps for fiction**, **unconfirmed facts about real things**, and
**what the cord can measure**.

---

## Two findings before the detail

**1. The same shape of gap appears twice, and neither is invented.**

We already have thirty-five bronze bars dispersed to "the cities of London,
Edinburgh and Dublin, the United States and other countries," with no register.

The Temeraire gives us the identical shape. When she was broken up at
Rotherhithe in 1838, the shipbreaker Beatson had **twelve dining chairs** made
from her timbers. His brother William took **two** to New Zealand when he
emigrated in 1851, and **one is in the Whanganui Regional Museum today.**

Nobody says where the other ten went.

Thirty-five bars, ten chairs. Two real dispersals, both documented up to the
point where the record stops, both leaving objects loose in the world that
someone could claim to hold. **That rhyme is free and it is true.**

**2. The best gap in either subject is twenty-eight years wide.**

The Eleanor Cross at Charing was built 1291–94 and pulled down in **1647**. The
statue of Charles I went up on the site in **1675**.

Twenty-eight years, no monument, a Commonwealth and a Restoration in between —
and then somebody put a statue back on "the site of the original cross" and
every road sign in Britain has counted from it ever since.

**Nobody surveyed it. How did they know where to put it?**

That is a documented hole in the record of the country's own datum point, and
it sits at the exact centre of a story about measurements nobody checked.

# FILL 1 · CHARING CROSS

## The facts

- **The statue** is by **Hubert Le Sueur**, probably cast in **1633**, during
  Charles's lifetime.
- After the Civil War it was sold to **John Rivett**, a metalsmith in Holborn,
  with instructions from Parliament to break it up. **He hid it instead** — it
  is said he buried it — **produced broken brass as evidence of compliance, and
  sold brass-handled cutlery to Royalists and Parliamentarians alike, claiming
  it was made from the statue.**
- Erected on its present site in **1675**, on a pedestal designed by Wren.
- **The site** is that of the last and most elaborate of the Eleanor crosses,
  built **1291–94** by Edward I for Eleanor of Castile, standing about three and
  a half centuries until **1647**.
- **London's Point Zero** is a plaque in the footpath a few feet behind the
  statue.
- The plaque records, in substance, that the original Queen Eleanor's cross
  stood on the site now occupied by the statue, that a replica stands in front
  of Charing Cross station, and that **mileages from London are measured from
  the site of the original cross**.
- Charing Cross has been the notional centre of London since the early
  nineteenth century, and **many laws and regulations defined their area from
  it**. Road distances are still officially measured from it.
- **The Victorian replica in front of the station stands about 203 metres —
  222 yards — from the original site.**

## The gaps a fiction can live in

| | |
|---|---|
| **The twenty-eight years** | 1647 to 1675, with nothing on the site and nobody measuring. The strongest gap in either subject |
| **The 222 yards** | Two objects both called Charing Cross, a furlong apart, and the country measures from one of them. Which one, and who decided |
| **Rivett's brass** | A documented case of metal sold under a false provenance. Nobody knows how much, or where any of it went. **A story about whether a piece of metal is what it claims to be, in 1650** |
| **Who carved the pedestal** | See below |

## Type 2 — real things, not entirely confirmed

These are the ones to be careful with, and the ones most useful to us.

1. **Who carved the pedestal.** Sources split: Wren designed it and **Grinling
   Gibbons** carved it, versus the carved work was executed by **Joshua
   Marshall**. Both attributions are in print. **An unresolved attribution on a
   real object at the country's datum point** is exactly the register we want.
2. **Whether Rivett buried it.** Every account hedges — *it is said he buried
   it*. The hiding is accepted; the burial is folklore.
3. **Whether the statue is precisely on the cross's site.** Universally
   asserted, nowhere evidenced. See the twenty-eight years.
4. **The plaque's exact wording, and whether it is one plaque or two.** Sources
   describe a Point Zero plaque in the footpath *and* an Eleanor Cross plaque by
   the statue. **They may be the same object or two different ones — nobody has
   checked.** Field item.

## Leads to follow

**On the ground**
- Photograph both plaques, if there are two. Record exact wording, exact
  positions relative to the pedestal.
- Is the island reachable safely, and is the plaque legible standing up?
- Measure the pedestal base and note any bench marks or survey marks cut into
  the stonework — datum points attract them.

**Online**
- *Survey of London*, vol. 16, has a chapter on the statue and the site of
  Charing Cross. It is on British History Online, free, and will settle the
  pedestal attribution and probably the 1675 siting.
- Historic England's listing for the statue — listing texts are detailed and
  frequently record what other sources do not.
- London Remembers has separate entries for the statue and for the cross.

# FILL 2 · THE FIGHTING TEMERAIRE

## The facts

- **NG524**, oil on canvas, **90.7 × 121.6 cm**, painted 1839.
- **Turner refused offers to buy it**, called it *his Darling*, kept it until he
  died in 1851, and it reached the nation in the **Turner Bequest of 1856**.
- **The masts were already gone.** Navy Board records: upper masts removed at
  Sheerness **July 1838**, lower masts the month after — before the tow. Turner
  painted her fully rigged and riding high.
- **The sun is on the wrong side.** She is towed *west* up the Thames from
  Sheerness, so the setting sun should be ahead of her.
- **Two tugs, not one.** And there was a **full moon** that night, not the new
  moon he painted.
- **Nobody remarked on the sun until James Dafforne in 1877** — thirty-eight
  years after it was painted, by which time it was already one of the
  best-loved pictures in the country.
- She was broken up at **Rotherhithe by the Beatsons**.
- **Her timbers survive.** A communion table and two bishop's chairs, made 1838
  for **St Paul's Rotherhithe**, the Beatsons' local church. When St Paul's was
  demolished in **1955** the furniture went to **St Mary's Rotherhithe**, the
  parish church, along with columns and altar rails.
- **Twelve dining chairs.** William Beatson took two to New Zealand in 1851.
  **One is in the Whanganui Regional Museum.**
- **The £20 note.** The polymer twenty entered circulation on 20 February 2020
  carrying Turner's self-portrait and The Fighting Temeraire. **139 × 73 mm.**

## The gaps a fiction can live in

| | |
|---|---|
| **The ten missing chairs** | Twelve made, two accounted for, ten unrecorded. The thirty-five bars again, in oak |
| **1955** | A church demolished, its furniture moved to another church. A transfer with no reason to be inventoried — the same shape as the 1900 vestry dissolution |
| **Whether Turner was there** | It is not established that he witnessed the tow. What is *not* in the sketchbooks is a gap, and the sketchbooks are catalogued |
| **Thirty-eight years** | Between painting and anybody noticing the sun. Nobody has explained why |

## Type 2 — real things, not entirely confirmed

1. **Whether Turner saw the tow.** Disputed. Widely assumed.
2. **Whether the errors are licence or ignorance.** Every commentator asserts
   deliberate artistic licence. **Nobody can actually know**, and the
   distinction is unfalsifiable — which makes it perfect type 2 ground.
3. **How many chairs, and whether the St Mary's furniture is all genuine
   Temeraire oak.** Provenance of relic timber is notoriously soft, and
   Rotherhithe was a shipbreaking town full of old ship's oak.
4. **Which timbers went into what.** Sources list columns, chairs, a table,
   altar rails — with no single authority.

## Leads to follow

**On the ground**
- **St Mary's Rotherhithe.** The communion table, the bishop's chairs, the
  altar rails. Off-route — about four kilometres east — so a lead and a
  possible book chapter, **not a clue site**. Worth a visit.
- **The National Gallery, NG524.** Free, on the route, at the part we already
  have. Note the hanging position and whether the frame carries a plate.

**Online**
- The National Gallery's own catalogue entry — Egerton's 2000 British Paintings
  catalogue is online and will be the authority on the errors and the
  provenance.
- Tate's Turner Bequest is fully digitised, sketchbook by sketchbook. **The
  question of what he drew that night is answerable by anyone with a browser**,
  which is exactly why we must not invent anything about it.
- Whanganui Regional Museum's online collection for the chair.

# What the cord should measure

The rule from `MECHANIC.md`: **never align the cord to the target's own extent**
— that leaves a fraction any photograph yields. **Anchor on a named physical
point, land at an absolute offset, and ideally anchor on one object and land on
another.**

Against that, ranked:

### 1. Point Zero, anchored on the kerb — the best target on the route
Zero on a named corner of the traffic island kerb; the knot lands on a letter of
the plaque set in the footpath. **Two objects, no shared photograph, no ratio to
recover, both permanent, both at ground level.** And the meaning is exact: you
are using a length to find a letter at the point the country measures from.

### 2. The pedestal, anchored low and turned round a corner
The Wren pedestal has arrises. **Running the cord round a corner is the defence
a photograph cannot beat**, because a flat image cannot see two faces at once or
measure the wrap. Portland stone, permanent, reachable.

### 3. The £20 note — the painting, in your pocket
**139 × 73 mm**, published, identical in every wallet, and it carries the
Temeraire. A cord station that needs no monument at all, cannot be damaged, and
works in the rain.

Two caveats. It is the one target where the dimensions *are* published, so the
proportion attack applies — use it only with an anchor **off** the note, or for
a step where the answer is not sensitive. And not every team will have one; the
pack should include one, or the step must be optional.

### 4. The terrace brass — already in the design
Unchanged, and still the physical heart of it.

### Explicitly not a cord target
**The painting.** It is behind a barrier, it must never be approached with a
piece of string, and the gallery would be entirely right to stop anyone trying.
The Temeraire earns its place through its explainer, not through measurement.

## What this changes

**Charing Cross is no longer just a fill.** It has a datum plaque, a
twenty-eight-year hole in its own provenance, a documented case of fraudulent
metal, an unresolved attribution, and two objects a furlong apart both claiming
the name. It may be the single richest location on the route.

**And the Temeraire chairs give the story its second unregistered dispersal.**
Ten objects made from a famous thing, loose in the world, with the record
stopping mid-sentence. We did not have to invent either one.
