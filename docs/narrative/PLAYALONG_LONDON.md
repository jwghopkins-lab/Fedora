# GROUND TRUTH — the London play-along

A walking version of the book, played as **Helen and Leo**, using **Arthur's
diary** as the physical object. Same premise underneath; the walk does not have
to explain it. Ends by naming **Smyth** and pointing at **Edinburgh**.

---

## The adaptation principle

**The book is global. The walk is one man's fieldwork.**

Arthur Pendelton died. The teams have his diary. Arthur was not writing a
thriller — he was walking London with a cord and a question: **why did the yard
change, and who changed it?** The diary is his working notebook: measurements,
rubbings, sketches, crossings-out, dead ends, and marginalia that go somewhere
much larger than a walk.

**That marginalia is where the premise lives.** The light-speed sequence,
Newton's cubit, the metre, Giza — these appear as things Arthur was chasing at
the edges of the page. **Never solved, never explained, always present.** A team
finishes the day having proved something small and true, and having glimpsed
something enormous they cannot yet reach. That is the correct ending for v1 and
it is also, conveniently, honest: the small local thing is the part that holds
up.

**Consequence worth stating plainly:** the walk never has to carry the plot's
weakest joints — the tectonic array, the 3-millisecond skim, the Triumvirate.
It carries the strongest ones, which are all physical and all in London.

---

## The premise as the teams meet it

Arthur's last entry says he had found the join, and that he had been careful
never to write the name down. **The walk is the reconstruction of the name.**

Everything the teams need is real, public, and outdoors — except one warm room
with a café in it, deliberately placed at the halfway point.

## CH.1 · WESTMINSTER — *The Night the Yard Burned*

**The question:** what was actually lost in 1834?

| Site | Access | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Westminster Bridge, north end** | Free, 24/7 | Turner watched the fire from the river. The vantage is the chapter's opening image |
| **Westminster Hall exterior / Old Palace Yard** | Free | The one large thing that survived |
| **The Jewel Tower** | Free outside, EH ticket inside | **The Standards Department worked here 1869–1938 and left because road-traffic vibration made precise measurement impossible.** The single best fact in the chapter |
| **Elizabeth Tower** | Free, external | Airy set its accuracy standard; **the rate is trimmed by stacking old pennies on the pendulum — one penny ≈ 0.4 sec/day.** Measurement as a physical, adjustable, slightly absurd act |

**Puzzle spine — the tally.** The fire started in burning centuries of Exchequer
**tally sticks** — notched wood, the oldest English recording-and-measuring
system. **Arthur's cord is knotted like a tally**, and the diary teaches the
notation here. Everything the cord does later is set up in this chapter.

**Fun beats:** counting something architectural and reading it as notches;
standing where Turner stood; the vibration fact landing as *the state could not
measure because of lorries*.

## CH.2b · TRAFALGAR SQUARE — *The Standards on the Wall*

**The question:** what replaced what burned — and is it right?

| Site | Access | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Imperial Standards, north terrace wall** | **Free, 24/7** | **The heart of the whole hunt.** Public brass measures — yard, foot, inch, link, chain, perch, pole — set into the terrace. **This is where the cord is calibrated against the actual public standard** |
| **Charing Cross / statue of Charles I** | Free | **The point from which all distances to London are measured**, on the site of the original Eleanor Cross |
| **35 St Martin's Street** (Westminster Reference Library) | Free, external plaque | **Newton lived here.** Two minutes from the standards |
| **St Martin-in-the-Fields crypt** | **Free, indoor, café** | **The floor is old gravestones.** Warm, dry, readable, and the natural halfway rest |

**Puzzle spine — calibrate, then catch an error.** The diary's instruction is:
*cut nothing until you have laid the cord on the wall.* Teams calibrate on the
terrace, then use the calibrated cord on something that **does not come out
right**. The discrepancy is the chapter's payoff and the walk's proof-of-concept.

**The T4 gift, already in our verification queue:** the Newton plaque's dates are
**not agreed between sources** (1710–1727 vs 1710–1725). A team that checks finds
two answers and no resolution — **the record contradicting itself, on the street,
in one minute.** That is exactly the effect T4 was invented for, and it costs
nothing.

**The Edinburgh seed, planted here:** Charing Cross is where distances are
measured *from*. Arthur's diary has a page of distances to British cities in his
own hand. **One is wrong.** The wrong one is Edinburgh. Nobody needs to notice
yet; it pays at the end.

## CH.2 · BLOOMSBURY — *Four Thousand Weights*
### the pyramid research chapter

**The question:** what is a cubit actually, and who has one?

**The site is the [Petrie Museum of Egyptian and Sudanese Archaeology](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-collections/petrie-museum-egyptian-and-sudanese-archaeology), Malet Place, UCL.** Free, no booking.

**It could not be more exactly ours if we had built it.** Petrie is already the
hinge of the book — the believer's son who went to Giza to confirm the pyramid
inch and disproved it. **This is his collection**, and the specific thing it is
famous for is **metrology**:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Over 4,000 Egyptian weights** | The basis of his *Ancient Weights and Measures* (1926). **The objects he examined are the objects in the cases** |
| **Cubit rods** | ~52.5 cm, quadrangular, ruled in digits and palms. **The physical thing Newton spent years trying to reconstruct from books — in a case, in Bloomsbury, for free** |
| **Eight identified weight standards** | Not one standard. **Eight.** The ancient world could not agree either |

**The puzzle answer is a disagreement, and it is Petrie's own.** He recorded that
his rods did not match the accepted figure:

> *“the lesser cubit is usually stated to be 24 digits, it is distinctly limited
> as not over 23 digits on the two most detailed cubits”*

**So the answer is 23, where every book says 24.** A team standing at the case
can see the man who disproved one false measure quietly recording that another
one is off by a digit. **That is the entire hunt in a single object**, it is
verifiable in a minute, and we did not have to invent a word of it.

**Mechanic:** the diary carries a **stencil of a cubit rod's divisions**. Laid
against the real rods it does not fit — and the amount by which it fails is the
number the team needs. The cord measures length; **the stencil measures
disagreement.** This is the clearest justification the two-tool system has had.

### One thing we must handle rather than dodge

**Petrie was a eugenicist**, a close associate of Galton and Pearson, and his
racial theory ran directly through his archaeological interpretation. **UCL has
publicly reviewed whether the museum should keep his name**, and some propose
naming it instead for **Amelia Edwards**, who founded the collection.

**Teams will read this on UCL's own panels, so the diary should get there
first.** And it does not damage the theme — it sharpens it. Our Petrie is *the
man who checked, and was freed of a delusion.* The fact that he checked one
thing rigorously and **stayed catastrophically wrong about another** is not a
blemish on that idea. **It is the idea.** Rigour is local. It does not
generalise, it does not make you good, and believing it does is how the whole
Smyth disaster happened in the first place.

*Also worth the diary's attention: the woman founded it, the man's name is on
the door. A story about whose measurements got credited has no business
stepping over that.*

## CH.3 · PICCADILLY — *The Room Where It Was Not Read*

**Free, outdoors, and eight minutes from Trafalgar Square.**

**The Royal Society was at [Burlington House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burlington_House), Piccadilly, from 1857 until 1967.**
**Which means 7 February 1874 happened here** — the paper denied a reading, the
Fellowship resigned, in this courtyard, through that door.

**And the courtyard supplies its own irony, free.** In **1874** — the same year —
**the Royal Astronomical Society and the Society of Antiquaries moved into the
new Burlington House wings.** The RAS is still there today. So Smyth walked out
of one learned society in this courtyard **in the very year another one he
belonged to moved in.** He did not leave the building. He left the room.

**This is the strongest possible home for the ending**, and it converts an
abstract deduction into somewhere you can stand.

## CH.4 · THE RIVER — *Leo's Chapter*

**Justified by character:** Leo is a maritime logistics broker. **The water is
his.** This is the only stretch where he leads and Helen follows.

| Option | Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Thames Clipper, Embankment → Greenwich** | ~45–60 min | Scenic, seated, warm, costs a fare. Best atmosphere |
| **DLR via Canary Wharf** | ~25 min | Cheap, fast, and Docklands is where the book's Apex lives |
| **DLR to Island Gardens + Greenwich foot tunnel** | ~30 min | **You walk under the river.** Strongest single sequence of the three |

**On the way, free and unmissable: Cleopatra's Needle.** A genuine ancient
Egyptian obelisk on the Embankment — **the Giza thread standing in London,
outdoors, at no cost.** It carries **shrapnel scars from a 1917 bomb with a
plaque that says so**, a time capsule beneath it, and **two sphinxes installed
facing the obelisk rather than outward** — a documented oddity, i.e. free T4.

**Puzzle spine — the crossing.** The meridian crosses the Thames east of the
Dome. **The answer is a time, not a word:** note the minute you cross zero. It
cannot be pre-solved, cannot be looked up, and it makes the journey the puzzle
rather than an interval between puzzles.

## CH.5 · GREENWICH — *Where He Lost*

**Everything needed is outside the paid gate.**

| Site | Access | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Shepherd Gate Clock** | **Free, 24/7, on the outside wall** | 24-hour dial, GMT — the first clock to show the public the time the country actually ran on |
| **Public Standards of Length, in the wall beside it** | **Free, 24/7** | **The same measures as Trafalgar Square.** The cord calibrated at Charing Cross is verified here. The rhyme closes |
| **The Time Ball, Flamsteed House** | **Free to watch** | **Rises 12:55, drops 13:00.** A hard, public, daily event |
| **The meridian line and Airy's transit circle** | Ticketed | Optional. Not required to finish |

**The chapter's fact:** Airy won here. He fixed the meridian, he regulated the
clock, and he is the reason the country's time came out of this hill.

## The ending — Smyth, then Edinburgh

Arthur never wrote the name. The last legible page has **a date and a phrase**:

> **7 Feb. 1874 — "denied a reading."**

Not rejected. **Not heard.** Teams resolve the date to the man: the paper that
was never read, and the Fellowship resigned the same day. **The name is Smyth**
— the only figure who appears in all three chapters at once: he fought the yard,
he fought the meridian, and he lost both to the men whose instruments the teams
have been standing next to all day.

**And Smyth's post was in Edinburgh.** His papers were inventoried at the Royal
Observatory there. The distance page from Charing Cross pays off. The hunt ends
with a direction and a distance, not an answer — **which is what leaves room for
v2.**

## What we cut, and why

| | |
|---|---|
| **Teddington / NPL** | User's call, and correct — no public access |
| **The Royal Mint** | Newton's Mint was inside the Tower. Ticketed, far east, breaks the geography |
| **Westminster Abbey / Newton's tomb** | £30 and a bottleneck. **Newton is better served free, at his own house by Leicester Square** — see decision 3 |
| **Giza, the ship, the Levant** | Book only |
| **The British Museum** | Holds cubit rods and opens seven days — **the fallback if the Petrie's hours don't fit.** But it is vast, crowded and almost impossible to write a precise clue into |
| **Sir John Soane's Museum** | **The sarcophagus of Seti I**, free, and the most atmospheric room in London. Timed entry and tiny capacity kill it for a team hunt — **hold it for v2** |
| **Freemasons' Hall** | Free, on-theme, and *exactly* the standard-issue conspiracy furniture our whole type system exists to avoid |

## The diary as an object

Not a puzzle book. **A working notebook** — pasted rubbings, pressed cord,
crossed-out arithmetic, three pages torn out, and marginalia in a second, later
hand. Puzzles live *in the mess*, which is both more atmospheric and much harder
to strip-mine than numbered questions.

**Tools:** the **cord** (knotted, uncalibrated until Trafalgar), and **stencils
or overlays** — transparent sheets that register onto plaques and standards to
read something that is not visible unaided. The cord is for length; the stencils
are for alignment. Two tools, two verbs, no bloat.
