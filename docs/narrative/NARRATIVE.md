# GROUND TRUTH — the story

The narrative worked as a book, not a walk. Same discipline either way: the
route only tells us which characters and objects we can physically stand in
front of.

---

## The three types

| | | rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Type 1 — FACT** | Verifiable, published, checkable in a minute. | The only thing a player or reader may be asked to *stand on*. Every chain reaches one within three hops. |
| **Type 2 — READING** | An interpretation of type 1. *"A man who left his own name inside every bronze he made was doing more than signing it."* Not a claim about the record, so search cannot refute it. | Where the fiction actually lives. Unlimited use. |
| **Type 3 — INVENTED** | Made up outright. The clerk, the researcher, the cord, the letter. | May only ever be an **edge between two type 1 nodes**. Never a terminal, never a fact a reader is asked to believe on its own. |

The red-team result that forces this: every fabrication style scored **zero**
corroborations across five blind verifiers, and the tell was always
*"unverifiable"*, never *"false"*. A reader needs only ask *which of these can
you confirm?* So we never assert an invented fact. We assert true ones and read
them.

## CORRECTION to D2 — and the corrected version is much better

**I had this backwards.** STATE.md said Britain was offered the meridian in
exchange for acceding to the Convention du Mètre "and Britain never did."

**Britain signed the Metre Convention on 17 September 1884.** The International
Meridian Conference opened in Washington on **1 October 1884** and gave
Greenwich the prime meridian, 22–1, on the 13th.

**Two weeks apart.** Britain did not break a promise. Britain **paid up front
and collected a fortnight later.**

That is a far better fact than the one I had. It is not a betrayal, it is a
*transaction*, and the timing is on the public record for anyone who lines the
two dates up — which nobody does, because the two events are filed under
different subjects in every history ever written.

And then the second half: **having signed, Britain did nothing.** The 1895
Select Committee recommended metric be compulsory within two years. The 1897
Act legalised it and did not compel it. Britain sat on that signature for
eighty years, until Douglas Jay stood up in 1965.

**So the shape is: signed to get Greenwich, then sat on it.** All type 1.

## Does it stack up to the present day? — honestly, not as it stands

**Nobody in 2026 cares whether the 1855 yard was three parts per million
short.** The yard has been *defined* as exactly 0.9144 m since 1959. The bar is
a museum object. If the modern stake is "the old yard was wrong", there is no
story, because the answer is "yes, and?"

Two mechanisms do carry to the present. Use both.

### 1. The error propagated into things still in service — type 1

Clarke's Royal Medal was for *"comparison of standards of length, **and**
determination of the figure of the earth"*. In Victorian geodesy that is one
job: **the shape of the Earth was computed from the length standards.**

- Clarke compared the standards at Southampton and published in **1866**.
- From that work came the **Clarke 1866 ellipsoid**, and the **Clarke 1880**.
- **Clarke 1866 is the basis of NAD27**, which is still the datum of record for
  older railways, irrigation canals and pipelines across North America,
  maintained on it deliberately to avoid re-surveying. It is in every GIS
  package on earth. **Clarke 1880 underpins several African and French datums
  still in use.**

So: a defect in the standards is not a historical curiosity. It is *in the
ground*, under infrastructure, in software, today. That is checkable, it is
astonishing, and it needs no invention at all.

### 2. Nobody protects the sin. They protect the concealment — type 2

This is how real cover-ups actually persist, and it is the honest answer to
"would anyone still care?" No institution defends a three-ppm error. Every
institution defends **the fact that it knew and did not say.** The thing with a
half-life measured in centuries is not the mistake; it is the minute of the
meeting where the mistake was noted and buried.

## The engine: let the crank be a crank

**This is the best idea in the whole story and it is almost entirely true.**

Piazzi Smyth believed the restored yard was wrong. He was *right that it was
wrong*, and **completely wrong about why** — he believed it because of divinely
ordained pyramid inches, which is nonsense.

So the establishment never had to lie. It could destroy him **truthfully**. The
Royal Society rejected his pyramid paper in **1874** on the merits, because the
merits were terrible. Everyone could see he had gone strange. He resigned his
Fellowship in **1888** and left the Royal Observatory in the same year, after
Airy's withering public criticism of Calton Hill.

**And in demolishing the crank, they buried the conclusion he had blundered
into.** A true thing protected by an obviously false version of itself.

No murders are required. No conspiracy meeting is required. It only requires
that a room full of clever men found it *convenient* not to check a lunatic's
arithmetic — which is the most plausible thing in this entire document.

## The Newton threat — why the crank could not simply be laughed off

The establishment's cleanest weapon against Smyth is *only a fool looks for
units in a pyramid*. That weapon has a defect, and it is type 1:

- John Greaves, Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford, surveyed Giza and
  published **Pyramidographia, 1646**. Newton owned a copy.
- **Isaac Newton wrote *A Dissertation upon the Sacred Cubit of the Jews*,**
  published 1737 in Greaves's *Miscellaneous Works*. He worked from Greaves's
  measurements to recover the Egyptian cubit, in order to recover the Hebrew
  sacred cubit, in order to reconstruct the Temple of Solomon for his
  chronology.
- Taylor's "pyramid inch" of 1859 is one twenty-fifth of that sacred cubit —
  **the cubit Newton postulated.**
- The manuscript is real, catalogued, and readable today.

**So the pyramid-inch idea is not fringe. Newton did it first, and the Royal
Society was rejecting a pyramid paper in 1874 while holding its own founding
god's.** That is the hinge of the whole book: the thing that makes the
establishment's contempt *unsafe* rather than merely unkind.

### Airy — the architect *(1801–1892)*
The antagonist, and the record supports it in a way it does not support Clarke.
He was **chairman and working secretary of the standards commission** that
rebuilt the yard, and he built the **Transit Circle that defined the meridian**.
*The same man fixed the nation's length and the world's longitude.* Both of his
monuments rest on the same foundation. And he was Smyth's real, documented,
personal antagonist. He has the motive, the position and the grudge. He dies in
1892, before it matters.

### Clarke — the tragedy, not the villain *(1828–1914)*
The man who did the actual comparing, and computed the Earth from it. **If the
yard is wrong, Clarke's life's work is wrong.** He is the victim of the
concealment, not its author — and the one person capable of finding it.

Three type 1 facts do enormous work here:
- **He resigned in 1881.** Twenty-seven years at Southampton, ordered abroad,
  and he resigned instead. Documented, and never explained.
- **Royal Medal in 1887**, the year before Smyth resigned his Fellowship.
- **He outlives all of them.** Airy 1892, Smyth 1900, Strachey 1908. **Clarke
  dies in 1914 — the last man alive who could have known.**

### Smyth — right by accident, destroyed truthfully *(1819–1900)*
Not murdered. He died at eighty-one at Ripon, twelve years out of the fight,
and killing him is both weaker and less defensible than what actually happened
to him. **He was ignored.** That is worse, and it is free.

### Strachey — the man at the table *(1817–1908)*
British commissioner at the 1884 conference and one of its secretaries. Father
of Oliver, father-in-law of **Ray Strachey**, whose name is on the plinth at
part 2. Dies 1908.

### The clerk — type 3 *(d. 1918)*
Vestry clerk of St Martin-in-the-Fields. Corresponded with Smyth in the 1880s;
his survey book ends in 1900 when the vestries are abolished and he is told to
hand over the parish's standards. Dies in the influenza.

### The researcher — type 3 *(now)*
Annotating the clerk's book. Missing.

## Plot points

### The historical thread
1. The fire takes the yard, and with it the physical answer to *how long*.
2. Airy rebuilds it from comparison and inference. The restored yard is very
   good and very slightly wrong.
3. Clarke, at Southampton, computes the shape of the Earth **from those
   standards**. The error enters the ground.
4. Smyth arrives at the right conclusion by the wrong road and says so loudly,
   in the language of divine revelation.
5. The Royal Society destroys him honestly, in 1874. Nobody has to lie.
6. Clarke, alone, sees what Smyth's noise is sitting on top of. *(type 3)*
7. **1881**: Clarke resigns rather than be posted abroad. *(type 1 event, type
   3 reason)*
8. **1884**: Britain signs the metre in September and takes the meridian in
   October. Whatever was known could not be said in that fortnight.
9. Smyth resigns everything in 1888, is dead by 1900, and the parish standards
   scatter in the same year.
10. Clarke keeps it until 1914 and takes it with him. *(type 3)*

### The modern thread
1. The researcher goes looking for the thirty-five bars, which is a real,
   documented dead end.
2. They find the clerk's survey book — a parish officer's working record with
   Smyth's letters pasted in. *(type 3)*
3. They realise the book is not about the bars. It is about **what a parish
   officer could check for himself, and stopped being allowed to.**
4. They start annotating, then measuring, then going out at odd hours.
5. They line up **17 September and 1 October 1884**, which nobody had.
6. They stop.

### What D14 + D15 do to each other — and the answer is very good

Taken together these two produce a problem and then solve it.

**The problem.** If the trigger is digitisation, then everything the researcher
used is *already public*. Newton's dissertation, Airy's account of the
standards, Clarke's comparisons, the 1884 conference proceedings, the vestry
records — all of it online, all of it citable, none of it suppressible. **You
cannot seize a document that is already on five servers.** So in the modern
half there is nothing to take, and with Airy dead in 1892 there is nobody
obvious to take it.

**The answer, which falls straight out of "both, layered".** The *facts* are
public and unsuppressible. What is not public is the **concealment record** —
the minute, the marginal note, the sheets Clarke asked to be kept together.
And the reason that has never been digitised is that it was never catalogued,
and the reason it was never catalogued is that it was **never officially a
document at all**.

It is a parish clerk's working survey book.

So: digitisation makes every fact in the story freely available, and leaves
exactly one thing outside the net — the one object that was never in an
archive because nobody ever thought it was worth putting in one. **That is the
book the players are carrying.** The trigger and the artifact turn out to be
the same idea seen from two ends.

### The consequence chain, which is what makes it matter

Clarke's Royal Medal was for *comparison of standards of length* **and**
*determination of the figure of the earth* — one job, because the Earth's shape
was computed **from** the standards. Clarke 1866 became the basis of NAD27,
still the datum of record for older railways, canals and pipelines across North
America and present in every GIS package; Clarke 1880 underpins several African
and French datums. Every link is type 1 and checkable in minutes.

So the reader is never asked to believe an error moved a border. They are shown
that **the standards and the shape of the Earth were the same computation**, and
left to sit with it.

### Still open, and it is now the sharpest question in the book

**Who acts in the modern half, and why?** D14 says the concealment record is
what makes someone act — but D15 has removed the obvious lever. Candidates:

1. **Somebody whose current work depends on it.** A boundary case, an
   infrastructure body, a survey still being maintained on NAD27 precisely to
   avoid re-surveying. Concrete, and it makes the stake financial and legal
   rather than reputational.
2. **Institutional reputation.** A body that would rather the minute stayed
   uncatalogued. Plausible, but thin motive for anything dramatic.
3. **Nobody acts at all.** The researcher is not being pursued. They stopped
   for a human reason and the reader supplies the conspiracy — which is exactly
   what the whole book is about, and would be the bravest ending available.
   It also rhymes with Nelson's Column: wrong for 150 years because everybody
   copied everybody, and nobody was to blame.

This folds into **D11**, which is still open.

## One document

**Type 3 — invented, and it is an *edge*.** It sits between two type 1 nodes
(Clarke's 1881 resignation, and the fact that the comparisons were made under
Sir Henry James at Southampton) and asserts nothing checkable.

Note what it does **not** do. It does not name a figure. It does not claim an
event. It does not say anything a search could contradict — every sentence is
either a matter of the writer's own state of mind, or a request.

>
> Sir,
>
> I am obliged for yours of the 27th and for the consideration it shows, which
> I do not underestimate.
>
> I must nevertheless decline. I am fifty-two and have been at this table since
> I was twenty-two, and I do not think I would do the Department credit abroad.
> You will understand that this is not modesty. A man who has spent thirty
> years being careful about very small quantities is not much use in a place
> where the quantities are large and the season is short.

>
> A. R. Clarke
