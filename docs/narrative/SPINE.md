# GROUND TRUTH — the spine

The writing document. Everything is decided; this is what to write and in what
order. Companion to `NARRATIVE.md` (why) and `CAST.md` (who).

---

## The structure, and why it has to be this one

D18 says nobody acts. There is no chase, no pursuit, no antagonist in the
modern half. That removes the standard engine, so the structure has to supply a
different one.

**The modern thread is an act of reading.** Each modern chapter is the narrator
working out what to look at next; each historical chapter delivers it. They
alternate. The tension is not *will they be caught* — it is **the reader's
steadily growing certainty that someone is going to come**, which is a debt the
book deliberately never pays.

That means one rule governs the whole middle:

> **Every historical chapter ends on a turn** — a fact that reframes the chapter
> before it. Not a cliffhanger. A recontextualisation. With no chase, the
> forward motion is entirely made of *oh, so that's what that was*.

The turns are listed per chapter below. If a chapter has no turn, it is not a
chapter yet.

---

## Voice

Written to be read aloud. Test every paragraph that way.

- **Short sentences carry the facts. Long ones carry the readings.** If a
  sentence is doing both, split it.
- **Never explain the significance of a fact you have just stated.** State the
  next fact instead and let the reader do it. The material is strong enough;
  underlining it is what makes this kind of book feel cheap.
- **No rhetorical questions.** Not one.
- **Plain nouns.** *Brass, drawer, willow, nettles, hat, string.* The subject is
  abstract enough without abstract language on top of it.
- **Dates bare.** "In 1866 Clarke published." Not "It was in the year 1866 that
  Clarke would publish."
- **The narrator is not clever.** They are careful. Cleverness in the narration
  competes with the material.

---

## The type discipline, applied line by line

Every chapter gets an audit before it is finished. Go through it and mark each
claim:

| | |
|---|---|
| **T1** | Verifiable. Someone could check it in a minute and find you right. |
| **T2** | A reading of a T1. Cannot be checked because it is not a claim about the record. |
| **T3** | Invented. |

Then apply the test: **every T3 must sit between two T1s and touch nothing on
its own.** If a T3 is the last thing in a paragraph, or the reason a reader
believes something, it is load-bearing and must be rebuilt.

Target ratio per chapter: **roughly 70 / 20 / 10.** If T3 is over 15% the
chapter has drifted into invention; if T1 is over 85% it has become a lecture.

## The chapters

### PART ONE — THE BOOK

**1 · The book arrives**
The survey book, two hands, and the modern one stopping mid-sentence.
*Carries:* the object, the two registers, and the fact that the Victorian hand
is a nobody.
*Turn:* the marginalia is not commentary. It is instructions.
*Types:* almost entirely T3 — this is the one chapter where that is allowed,
because nothing in it is asserted as history.

**2 · Sixteen October 1834**
Turner on the river. The tally sticks in the furnace under the Lords. The
standard yard in a room nobody thought about.
*Carries:* the founding loss, and the fact that it was caused by burning an
obsolete system of measurement.
*Turn:* nobody in the crowd was thinking about the standards, because there was
no reason to. Rooms do not usually go anywhere.

**3 · What the book is**
The narrator works out that it is a parish officer's working record, not a
diary. Vestry, local standards, comparison certificates.
*Turn:* the parish had its own yard, and somebody used to walk out and check
the shops with it.

### PART TWO — THE RECORD

**4 · The room where it was rebuilt**
Airy's commission, 1838–42. Forty bars from Troughton & Simms. Four
Parliamentary Copies. Thirty-five dispersed to "the cities of London, Edinburgh
and Dublin, the United States and other countries."
*Turn:* there is no register of the thirty-five. Every source stops at that
sentence.

**5 · Southampton**
Clarke at the Ordnance Survey Office, 1850–81. The 1866 comparisons.
*Turn:* his medal was for comparison of standards of length **and**
determination of the figure of the earth — because they were the same job. The
shape of the Earth was computed out of a case of brass bars.

**6 · The crank**
Smyth. Tenerife, where he was right. The 1864 book. The 1874 rejection.
*Turn:* they rejected the reasoning, and the reasoning deserved it. But *the
yard is wrong* and *the yard is wrong because of divine revelation* are two
statements, and only one of them was ever tested.

**7 · What Newton wrote**
Greaves at Giza in 1646. Newton's *Dissertation upon the Sacred Cubit*,
published 1737 — the Egyptian cubit, to get the Hebrew one, to rebuild the
Temple. Taylor's pyramid inch is one twenty-fifth of Newton's cubit.
*Turn:* the Royal Society rejected a pyramid paper in 1874 while holding its
own founding god's.
*This is the hinge of the book. Write it fourth or fifth, not late.*

**8 · Letters to clerks**
Smyth's private survey by post. What is a local standard, when was it last
compared, what did it read. Dozens of parishes. Careful, dull, real work.
*Turn:* he was doing proper fieldwork the whole time, for a reason that was
nonsense, and nobody ever collated it because of who was asking.

**9 · A fortnight in 1884**
17 September: Britain signs the Metre Convention. 1 October: the Meridian
Conference opens in Washington. 13 October: Greenwich, 22–1.
*Turn:* two weeks. Britain paid in September and collected in October, and the
two events are filed under different subjects in every history ever written.

**10 · 1881**
Clarke, twenty-seven years at one table, ordered abroad, resigns instead.
*Turn:* three years before the fortnight. He was gone before it happened.
*Types:* the event is T1 and the reason is T3. Keep the reason in the
conditional. Never state it.

**11 · Beating the bounds**
Rogationtide. Willow wands. Boys beaten at the marks so the boundary would hold
in court. The man who won a case aged seventy-five because he had been thrown
into nettles sixty years earlier.
*Turn:* where a boundary was disputed, the Ordnance Survey's own surveyors fell
back on the memories of men who had been beaten as children.

**12 · 1900**
Smyth dies in February. The vestries go the same year. Twenty-eight boroughs
absorb the property of dozens of parishes in one movement. The clerk writes
twice about the case of brass in the back room and gets no reply.
*Turn:* nobody came for them. There was no refusal. He kept them because
keeping them required nothing.

**13 · The last man**
Airy 1892. Smyth 1900. Strachey 1908. Clarke 1914.
*Turn:* Clarke outlived all of them and published nothing.

### PART THREE — THE WALK

**14 · Assembling**
How the narrator got there: Newton's manuscript, Airy's account, Clarke's
comparisons, the 1884 proceedings, the vestry records — five archives, all
online, none of which had ever spoken to another.
*Turn:* none of it was hidden. It was only ever scattered.

**15–19 · The walk itself**
Whitehall, the terrace, the bust, the church, the lane. The cord. Each chapter
is one length of string against one real object, and one letter.
*Carries:* the physical present tense, after fourteen chapters of paper.
*Turn (running):* every stop offers a shape that looks like a pattern.

**20 · Southampton**
The word completes. They look up at the street sign.
*Turn:* it is a coincidence. The street is named for the Earls of Southampton
and has nothing to do with the Ordnance Survey.

**21 · The debrief**
What was true, what was a reading, what was made up. Delivered straight.
*Turn:* the reader has spent twenty chapters waiting for someone to come, and
nobody ever did — and they should be able to feel exactly when they started
expecting it.

## The order to write in

Not chapter order. Write the ones that set the register first, then the ones
that depend on it.

1. **Chapter 2 (1834)** and **chapter 6 (the crank)**. These fix the voice. If
   the register works in both — one all event, one all argument — it will work
   everywhere.
2. **Chapter 7 (Newton)**. The hinge. Write it early so everything can lean on
   it.
3. **Chapters 5, 9, 11** — Southampton, the fortnight, the bounds. The three
   strongest turns. Get them down while they are fresh.
4. **Chapter 1**, once the historical voice exists, so the modern voice can be
   built deliberately against it.
5. **Everything else in order.**
6. **Chapter 21 last**, and write it honestly. It is the only chapter that gets
   to say what the book is for.

---

## The three things most likely to go wrong

1. **The middle sags** because there is no antagonist. The defence is the turn
   at the end of every historical chapter. Check them before writing, not after.
2. **The narration explains itself.** The single strongest instinct to resist.
   The material does the work; commentary makes it feel like a pitch.
3. **T3 creeps.** The clerk is so useful that he will start carrying facts. He
   must never carry one. Audit every chapter.
